Surface Deformation of California from InSAR and GNSS

California deforms across the plate boundary, over pumped aquifers in the Central Valley, and in earthquakes and slow aseismic events. Sentinel-1 radar interferometry maps this motion along the satellite's line of sight, and hundreds of GNSS stations measure it in three dimensions at points. We combine the two in one inversion: nine Sentinel-1 tracks from 2014 to 2026 and the GNSS positions together give east, north and up velocities and a displacement time series at any point.

The velocity is a joint collocation of the tracks' rate maps with the GNSS, on top of the compact vertical signals a U-Net recovers from the radar data: nonlinear, non-Gaussian features such as subsidence bowls and transients that a Gaussian collocation would smooth away. The time series model each term (rate, seasonal cycles, coseismic steps such as Ridgecrest 2019, and aseismic transients found in the radar data) as a three-dimensional field.

Coseismic Slip Models

Large earthquakes leave the slip on their faults imprinted in the displacement of the ground's surface. We invert space-geodetic measurements of that displacement for the distribution of slip on the faults that ruptured. The data are L-band interferometry from NISAR and ALOS-2, Sentinel-1 interferograms and pixel offsets, and GNSS. The models use layered elastic Earth models, meshes adapted to the data's resolution, and fault geometries tested against the data.

Planetary Exploration Twin: Enceladus

The Planetary Exploration Twin (PET) is a data-driven, modular framework that integrates science objectives, instrument design, resource management, and operational planning to assess data sufficiency and iteratively optimize mission parameters and concepts of operation. While applicable to any planetary body, my current focus is on Saturn's moon Enceladus and the use of interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) to measure crustal deformation.
